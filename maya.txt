1. “Unhappiness is our own invention. At times I'm sad that I lack the imagination for it.” Director Max Ophuls is true cinematic genius and The Earrings of Madame De.. showcases that with every scene. Having recently revisited Lola Montès I knew I wanted to continue seeing what this director has to offer and this film is like being transfixed by the radiant glint of a diamond. Even within the first 20 minutes you’re presented with some incredible one takes and tracking shots that I’ve rarely seen in a film of this era. How characters move through a scene with the camera flowing through crowds, up staircases, moving between focuses and then stop on an object perfectly, feels so smooth and magical. The pacing as well is barely noticeable as sequences end and move right into the next, It immediately made me think of Director Joe Wright’s incredible one takes and set pieces in Atonement and Pride and Prejudice . This is only my second film from Max Ophuls and I’m already super impressed. Who knew a simple story about a set of earrings could be so meaningful and symbolic as it takes the audience on an epic journey across countries and through the complicated lives of the elite, representing love, lies, affairs, secrets, control, fate and the mask of wealth. Its significance seems lost to the women who find it in their care, just as quickly as it dazzles them it’s treated like an ordinary commodity, selling it away only for it to return back where it started. The general and his wife are never actually named in the film, intentionally hidden from the audience just like their true intentions but like a loose mask it occasionally slips as they move about their world without consequence; much like the earrings they can’t linger too long physically and emotionally in one place or else they might openly show too much weakness or interest. It’s films like this that remind me of why I love cinema, how I could spend hours just diving into production essays and listening to commentaries about the special moments that could easily get missed. How a scene can take on a whole new meaning by noticing things like the space between actors, their separation by objects like doors, walls or paintings; the use of mirrors during a dining scene or how dialogue can still be playful and funny but still hint at a much deeper subtext. I just loved that subtle scene by the door where you can see a tear in Madame’s eye as she embraces the door and makes sure to stay behind it lest she give the game away to the Baron . Don’t even get me started about the letters she tosses that turn into snow.. Just a masterpiece from top to bottom, Bravo! “I've answered all your letters, my love. But I never had the courage to mail my replies.” -|| Watched on Criterion Channel ||-
2. Continuing down Kirsten Stewart’s amazing filmography I finally arrive at a film I’ve been eyeballing since I last watched Spencer . The Runaways , Directed by Floria Sigismondi , combines the kick ass duo of Fanning and Stewart , who are electric as they play to their natural personality archetypes as The Artist Cherie Currie ( Fanning ) and The Rebel Joan Jett ( Stewart ). A beautiful and mesmerizingly shot film that emphasizes attitude and style as The Runaways are formed alongside the eccentric record producer, Kim Fowley ( Michael Shannon ) who works to craft and chisel their respective talents into a bombastic rock ensemble. This film channels the intensity of the late 70’s music scene with its attention to detail in the sets and wardrobe that look to bury the disco age and signal the arrival of hard rock and metal. Is this Hollywood’s version of the birth of a legendary group? Oh, you betcha and honestly I’m not even mad, this film feels like a love letter to not just the band but distorted guitars and the rock and roll spirit. Any good story worth telling is a story worth embellishing and you couldn’t ask for a better cast. It’s a story told in dingy dressing rooms, bug infested hotels and sweat stained nightclubs. There’s a ton of hypnotic and dreamy scenes that show the effects of high drug use and living life at a hundred miles an hour, showing the toll touring can have on new groups. I honestly could talk about this film for days, I think it’s almost perfect, what I love most about music biopics is that it embodies the attitude and era that group or artist lived in, this one delivered that in spades, creating the world and space needed to let the actors perform. -|| Watched on Blu-ray ||-
3. The Body Snatchers is a 90’s fueled, slow-burn sci-fi, coming of age horror film that feels right at home alongside Director David Twohy’s The Arrival and Roger Donaldson’s Species . Directed by Abel Ferrara with a screenplay by Stuart Gordon , this is the third remake of the notable 1955 novel by Jack Finney that instead focuses its story on an Alabama military base where a small family is getting their footing. Marti ( Gabrielle Anwar ) is the first to encounter strange behavior as she is told “They get you in your sleep” from a frantic MP; as Steve ( Terry Kinney ) begins his work cleaning up hazardous chemicals around the base he immediately starts to suspect that something more is happening when a medical officer describes unusual symptoms he’s seeing in numerous patients who fear sleeping. It’s not long before the body horror starts as soldiers and civilians start getting transformed by the invaders. Right from the beginning you can feel the eerie-ness with the opening crawl, the unsettling music already setting the tone for the film. It has plenty of clever and self aware dialogue that you can’t help but love as many characters deliver subtle foreshadowing with plenty of double meanings. It’s a decent horror film overall that knows what is but definitely has its share of strange story decisions for example having the young boy in the family run around an active training area without anyone stopping him right away. Nowhere near perfect but a noble effort that can be pretty spooky and nasty at times. -|| Watched on Criterion Channel ||-
4. "Oh my god! You guys, I can't believe we made it." "Yea, we graduated high school.. how totally amazing." Two friends against the world, Ghost World is one of those nostalgia comedies you've known about for ages that just needed the right star alignment and phase of the moon to finally get to. Directed by Terry Zwigoff and based on a cult classic comic, feels like one of those films that's deeper than you suspect it is. Enid ( Thora Birch ) and Rebecca ( Scarlett Johansson ) just matriculated from high school and have entered their adult limbo phase, not quite sure what to do next but unwilling to hear suggestions. They end up finding themselves literally and metaphorically navigating the wacky and uncool world that surrounds them full of strange and hilarious encounters with struggling artists, lonely adults, goofy schoolmates and nerdy music collectors. It's a clever, funny and charming coming of age comedy that captures the heart of being thrust into the corporate hellscape that is adulthood in 2001. Grunge and punk are dead, hipsters are in and just about everyone is a bit too cynical for their own good; of course this film was released months before 9/11 so it really is a bit of a time capsule. This ended up being a fun diversion after all, no moon and star alignment required, a nostalgia trip that still feels all too relevant. "I remember this hat, this was during your little old lady phase." 😆 -|| Watched on Criterion Channel ||-
5. Focus Features film #15 - The Uprising , written and directed by Paul Greengrass , well known for the Bourne Trilogy takes a stab at a historical drama about the Wat Tyler Rebellion , a critical revolt that hurt the British war effort against the French during The Hundred Years War in 1381. Knowing a little of its historical relevance, I knew I would need to make time to see it. Andrew Garfield leads a stacked cast of excellent support actors who portray farmers and merchants decimated by plague and forced to pay higher and higher taxes to a desperate lordship. It has a solid, rugged and realistic looking production with a reluctant and mournful performance from Garfield that wastes no time getting right into the struggle. This is an emotional story about rage, mob instability and divine justice for the mistreatment of the laity. This is no Braveheart , no sprawling epic with grand sweeping cinematic vista shots and poetic speeches about living forever but a muddy and gloomy gathering of commoners seeking retribution. I can see why this film received the middling reviews it has, it’s a dingy, gray and more down to earth affair that feels much closer to Netflix’s The King , a film more about its themes and production than about giving time to character building. I’m personally fine with this approach, not every movie needs to be about Oscar winning cinematic moments. This is as grounded a tale as it might have actually been with the camera lying right in the mud with the unruly mob. This is a very respectable 3.5 for me atm and possibly upon a rewatch might raise it to 4. -|| Watched in Theater ||-
6. Tragic.. Aching.. Restorative.. Three Colors: Blue is a somber and compelling, psychological character study of grief, loss and one woman's path to resurrection. A film that blends color, texture, music, light and symbolism as Julie ( Juliette Binoche ) copes and struggles with the sudden death of her daughter and husband in an automobile accident. Written and Directed by Krzysztof Kieślowski , This is the first film in a famed series that seeks to address themes connected to the French political ideals represented in the nation's motto: liberté, egalité, fraternité . Blue focuses on the theme of liberation, how a person can get chained to pieces of their past and how letting love back into your world can free you. Julie isolates herself from her friends and family but over the course of the film she begins to speak to others again and becomes close to a man named Olivier ( Benoît Régent ) who wishes to help finish her husband's final music compositions. So many powerful, symbolic scenes, like her returning to swim in a pool of bright blue water with unfinished pieces of music continuing to intrude into her mind; Or when a witness to the accident reaches out to Julie to give her her husband's cross and last words, only later for the same cross to reappear on his mistress's neck. Passionately acted by the incredible Juliette Binoche , she delivers an excellent performance, an actress that is well known for her expressive elegance and range. While I wouldn't consider this is a full on masterpiece, it's still a superb example of European cinema that is well regarded for its deep themes and symbolic story. -|| Watched on Blu-ray ||-
